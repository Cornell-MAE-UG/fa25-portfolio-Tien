<h2> Q&A </h2>

What disk material can we use with the sap to have mock SLFs of a realistic weight to stick to?
- We are thinking of using wood chips or something similarly light to model SLFs. We can make the disk out of plastic, and to have the sap more effectively stick onto the disk, we can fabricate a shallow honeycomb surface on the disk.
How do different "rake" styles interact to scrape off SLFs without scraping off the sap?
- We think that a rake with densely packed metal tines (similar to a wire comb) will be able to push around SLFs without scraping off the sap from the disk. In our experimentation, we will work with different tine spacing to effectively scrape SLFs off the disk into the collection box.
How can we attach the crank to the disk to help rotate the rake?
- In our mockup, we made our manual crank offset a bit behind the actual disk to allow easy access and rotation. We did, however, run into a complication with positioning the disk over the SLF collection box. We can either position the disk directly over the doors of the box, or we can position the disk closer to the edge of the box, allowing SLFs to fall “forward” into the box. Whichever solution we choose will affect the offset distance of the manual crank.
How can we support the design to make it physically stable? Where would we put this solution in a real-world application (mount on stand vs. on tree)?
- Our mockup was a free-standing box, so we think it is appropriate to place our design on the ground or on a branch of the Tree of Heaven. On the ground, SLFs would be attracted to the disk if there is no Tree of Heaven nearby. On a Tree of Heaven branch, SLFs would already be there. In our mockup, we were able to support the disk on top of the collection box just by tape, but we are also considering having a free-standing base for the disk. In a refined prototype, we would need to consider secure attachment methods, such as welding, drilling, or reworking the design so the base of the disk can slot into part of the box. Our SLF collection box has a flat base, but to ensure that it doesn’t topple on a branch, we can use ropes to tie our design to the tree.


During the mock up, we realized that we can also test the effectiveness of different designs of the SLF collection system. We made one alternate version, which features a double funnel feature. 

<h2> Design Estimates </h2>
power, radius, height, length of box, length of crank.

*Power:*
Suppose we want our rake to make one full rotation once every minute. (We would have to learn more about SLF reaction time and behavior to make a better estimate.) This gives us angular velocity =260 s–1. If we have a 5 in (approx. 120 mm) long crank to spin our motor, and it takes a regular human push to spin the crank (approx. 100 N), this will result in 12 Nm of torque. Using P=, our baseline estimate is 1.3 W of power.

*Size:*
One SLF is about an inch long. Suppose one disk has the diameter of about 10 SLFs, or 10 inches. We expect this disk to be high enough for SLFs to fall off the disk into a collection box or funnel trap. Combined, the disk and box will be about two times the height of the disk. Lengthwise, the box must be at least the diameter of the disk. The depth of our design must consider both the depth of the box and base supporting the disk; we choose to approximate this as close to 1.5 times the diameter of the disk. In total, the space taken up by our design is a box with dimensions 101520 inches. In SI units, this is about 250380500 mm.
